When to See a Doctor About Fertility

The timelines for seeking help, what the first appointment involves, and the tests a doctor usually orders.

Knowing when to seek help with fertility is one of the most useful things to learn before you start trying, because the right timing saves months of uncertainty. The rules are simple and depend on your age and your cycle. This guide explains the timelines, what happens at the first appointment, and which tests are usually involved, so you can walk in prepared rather than worried.

The standard timelines

If you are under 35 and your cycles are regular, the usual advice is to seek help after a year of well-timed trying without success. If you are 35 or older, seek help after six months. These are not deadlines for failure; they are the points at which a check is likely to find something useful. Seek help sooner, without waiting, if your cycles are irregular, very painful, or absent, if you have a known condition such as endometriosis or polycystic ovary syndrome, or if you have had pelvic surgery, pelvic infection, or previous pregnancy difficulties.

What the first appointment covers

The first appointment is mostly a conversation. The doctor will ask about your cycle history, how long you have been trying, your general health, medicines, lifestyle, and your partner's health. Bring your cycle records: dates of periods, any temperature charts, mucus notes and ovulation test results. This history is genuinely useful, because it shows whether ovulation is happening and whether timing has been right. The doctor will also explain what tests are available and agree a plan with you, rather than ordering everything at once.

Tests for ovulation and hormones

The usual first tests check whether you are ovulating and whether your hormones are in the expected ranges. A blood test for progesterone, taken about a week after expected ovulation, can confirm ovulation happened. Other blood tests may check thyroid function, prolactin, and the hormones that control the cycle. An ultrasound can look at the ovaries and the uterus, and check for conditions such as polycystic ovaries or fibroids. These tests are simple, and most can be done without a hospital visit.

Tests for your partner

A semen analysis is one of the first tests offered, because it is simple, non-invasive, and answers a large question quickly. It measures sperm count, movement and shape, and it can be repeated if the first result is unclear. About a third of fertility difficulties involve the male partner alone, and another third involve both partners, so checking both sides from the start avoids months of focusing on the wrong cause. If the result is abnormal, the doctor can advise on next steps, which may include a referral to a specialist.

What happens next

Depending on the results, the next steps range from simple advice to treatment. Sometimes the answer is a small change, such as timing or a medicine to trigger ovulation. Sometimes it is a referral to a fertility specialist for further tests or treatments. It helps to go into the process with a plan: agree with your partner in advance how far you are willing to go, and take notes at each appointment. Fertility care works best when you understand the plan and feel able to ask questions, so never hesitate to ask what a test is for or what the result means.

What to bring to the appointment

Prepare for the first appointment by gathering four things. First, your cycle records: period start dates for the last six months, any temperature charts, mucus notes and ovulation test results. Second, a short history: how long you have been trying, how you timed it, and any previous pregnancies. Third, a list of medicines, supplements and health conditions, for both partners. Fourth, a list of questions: what tests are available, how long results take, and what the next steps are if the first tests are normal. Bringing these four things turns a vague conversation into a useful consultation, and it saves months of back-and-forth that could have been avoided.

Tip: Bring your cycle records to the first appointment — dates, temperature charts and test results are the evidence a doctor needs, and they save months of guesswork.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should we try before seeing a doctor?

A year if you are under 35, six months if you are 35 or older. Seek help sooner if cycles are irregular or you have a known condition.

What happens at the first fertility appointment?

Mostly a conversation about your cycles, health and how long you have been trying, plus simple blood tests and a semen analysis for your partner.

Is a semen analysis really necessary?

Yes, it is a simple first test. About a third of fertility difficulties involve the male partner alone, so checking both sides saves time.
