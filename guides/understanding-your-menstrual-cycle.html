Understanding Your Menstrual Cycle

What happens during each phase of the menstrual cycle, and how the phases connect to ovulation and fertility.

The menstrual cycle is the monthly rhythm that prepares your body for a possible pregnancy, and understanding it makes every fertility tool easier to use. It is driven by hormones, it has four recognisable phases, and its length is measured from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. Once you know what each phase does, you can read your own cycle with much more confidence, and you will know which days matter most for conception.

The menstrual phase: days 1 to 5

The cycle begins on the first day of full menstrual flow, when the lining of the uterus sheds because no pregnancy occurred in the previous cycle. Bleeding usually lasts three to seven days, and the amount and duration vary widely between people and even between months for the same person. Oestrogen and progesterone are at their lowest during this phase, which is why many people feel tired or low in energy. It is a normal part of the cycle, and a period that lasts far longer than seven days, or bleeding between periods, is worth mentioning to a doctor.

The follicular phase: before ovulation

While you are bleeding, the follicular phase is already under way. The brain releases follicle-stimulating hormone, which prompts several follicles in the ovaries to grow, and one usually becomes dominant. As that follicle grows, it releases oestrogen, which thickens the lining of the uterus and changes cervical mucus so it becomes clearer and more slippery. This phase is the variable part of the cycle: it can last anywhere from about a week to three weeks, and it is the reason cycle lengths differ. Stress or illness can lengthen it, which pushes ovulation later.

Ovulation: the middle of the cycle

Around the middle of the cycle, a surge of luteinising hormone triggers the dominant follicle to release its egg. That day is ovulation. The egg then travels down the fallopian tube, where it can be fertilised for roughly 12 to 24 hours. Some people feel a brief twinge of pain on one side, called mittelschmerz, and many notice a change in cervical mucus to a clear, stretchy texture around this time. The day of ovulation is the last day of the fertile window, not the first, because sperm needs to be present before the egg arrives.

The luteal phase: after ovulation

After the egg is released, the empty follicle becomes the corpus luteum and produces progesterone, which keeps the uterine lining ready for a possible pregnancy. This phase is the most consistent part of the cycle, usually lasting 12 to 16 days, and it is the reason ovulation can be estimated as 14 days before the next period. If no pregnancy occurs, progesterone falls, the lining sheds, and the cycle starts again. A luteal phase shorter than 10 days, repeated over several cycles, is worth discussing with a doctor.

What counts as a normal cycle

A cycle of 21 to 35 days is generally considered normal, with the average often quoted as 28 days. Variation of a few days from month to month is normal too, especially in the years after periods start and in the years before menopause. What matters more than hitting 28 days is your own pattern: a cycle that is usually 31 days is normal for you, even though it is longer than average. Track at least three cycles before drawing conclusions about your pattern, and keep a note of anything unusual, such as very heavy bleeding or severe pain.

Tracking your own phases

The best way to learn your cycle is to track it for three months with a simple notebook. Each day, note the date, whether you are bleeding, what your mucus looks like, and any pain or mood changes. After the first month, you will see the broad shape: bleeding days, then a quiet stretch, then slippery mucus, then a temperature rise if you chart it. After three months, compare the cycles: did ovulation fall on a similar day each time, or did it move? Did your luteal phase stay steady at 12 to 16 days? The answers tell you how regular you really are, and they give a doctor a clear history if you ever need one. A notebook and three months of notes are worth more than a year of guessing.

Tip: Your luteal phase is the most predictable part of your cycle — that is why ovulation is counted backwards from your next period, not forwards from your last one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a normal menstrual cycle?

Most cycles last 21 to 35 days. The average is often quoted as 28 days, but a regular 26-day or 32-day cycle is equally normal.

Why does my cycle length change?

The follicular phase varies in length with stress, illness, travel and exercise, while the luteal phase stays fairly steady. That is why ovulation moves around.

When am I most fertile in my cycle?

The five days before ovulation plus ovulation day itself. The two to three days before ovulation are usually the most fertile.
