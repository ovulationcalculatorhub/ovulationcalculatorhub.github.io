Ovulation Pain (Mittelschmerz) Explained

What ovulation pain feels like, why it happens, how to tell it from other pain, and when to see a doctor.

Mittelschmerz is the German word for middle pain, and it describes the brief ache some people feel on one side of the lower abdomen around ovulation. It is a real and common experience, though many people never feel it at all. It usually lasts minutes to hours, switches sides from month to month, and needs no treatment. This guide explains what causes it, how to recognise it, and when pain around ovulation is a sign of something else.

What it feels like

Ovulation pain is usually a dull ache or a sharp twinge on one side of the lower abdomen, on the side of the ovary that is releasing the egg. It can last from a few minutes to a day, and it sometimes comes with light spotting or a feeling of pressure. Some people feel it every month, some only occasionally, and many never feel it. Its timing is the clue: it appears around the middle of the cycle, near the expected ovulation date, and it fades on its own without treatment.

Why it happens

The exact cause is not fully settled, but the leading explanations are straightforward. As the follicle grows before ovulation, it stretches the surface of the ovary, which can cause a dull ache. When the follicle releases the egg, it also releases a small amount of fluid and sometimes a little blood, which can irritate the lining of the abdomen and cause a sharper pain. Both explanations fit the timing and the one-sided pattern, and neither suggests anything is wrong.

How to use it as a sign

If you notice the pain regularly, it can be a useful extra clue that ovulation is happening, especially when it lines up with your calendar estimate and with changes in cervical mucus. Record the side and the day each month, and after a few cycles you may see that it reliably appears a day or two before your temperature rises. But do not rely on it alone: many people feel nothing, and pain can be caused by other things. Treat it as supporting evidence, not as a timing tool on its own.

What helps

Most ovulation pain needs no treatment beyond rest and, if needed, an ordinary painkiller such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, following the packet instructions. A warm compress or a hot water bottle on the lower abdomen often helps, as does gentle movement. If you find the pain disruptive every month, talk to a doctor about options, which can include hormonal contraception to suppress ovulation. Keep a note of how long the pain lasts and how severe it is, because that record is useful if you ever discuss it with a doctor.

When pain needs medical attention

See a doctor promptly if pain around ovulation is severe, lasts more than two days, comes with fever, vomiting, or heavy bleeding, or if it is getting worse over months. Pain that is always on the same side, or that happens at other times of the cycle too, should also be checked, because it can point to an ovarian cyst, endometriosis, or pelvic infection. Sudden severe one-sided pain with dizziness or fainting needs urgent care, because it can rarely signal a ruptured cyst or, in early pregnancy, an ectopic pregnancy. Most ovulation pain is harmless, but severe or unusual pain deserves attention.

Recording ovulation pain usefully

If you feel ovulation pain, a short record makes it useful. Each month, note the date, which side the pain was on, how long it lasted, and how strong it was on a scale of one to five. After three months, look for the pattern: does it switch sides, does it arrive a day or two before your temperature rises, and does it line up with your calendar estimate? If the answers are yes, the pain is a reliable extra clue for you, and you can use it alongside mucus and tests. If the pain is always on the same side, lasts more than two days, or is getting stronger, take the record to a doctor, because the pattern is exactly the information that helps distinguish normal ovulation pain from something that needs checking.

Tip: Mittelschmerz switches sides from month to month, following whichever ovary releases the egg — pain that is always on the same side is worth mentioning to a doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does ovulation pain feel like?

A dull ache or sharp twinge on one side of the lower abdomen, lasting minutes to a day, around the middle of the cycle.

Does everyone feel ovulation pain?

No. Many people never feel it, and its absence means nothing about whether you ovulated.

When is ovulation pain a concern?

If it is severe, lasts more than two days, comes with fever or heavy bleeding, or keeps getting worse, see a doctor.
