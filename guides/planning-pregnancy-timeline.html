Planning a Pregnancy: A Simple Timeline

A month-by-month plan for preparing to conceive, from folic acid and health checks to timing and testing.

Planning a pregnancy is less about a single perfect month and more about a few months of steady preparation. The steps are simple: get your health in order, learn your cycle, and start timing well. Most of them cost little and make a real difference. This guide lays out a practical timeline you can follow, whether you want to start trying next month or next year.

Three months before trying

Start taking folic acid daily, ideally at least a month before conception, because it reduces the risk of neural tube defects in the baby's early development. Book a general health check and review any medicines you take, because some need to be changed before pregnancy. Check your vaccinations, particularly rubella, and your dental health. Stop smoking, and keep alcohol to a minimum. Begin tracking your cycles now, so that by the time you start trying you already know your average cycle length and your usual ovulation pattern.

One month before trying

Keep taking folic acid and continue tracking. If you use hormonal contraception, stop it now or as your doctor advises, and expect the first cycle or two to be irregular. Start watching cervical mucus so you can recognise your fertile pattern when it appears. Talk with your partner about the plan: how you will time things, how long you will try on your own before seeking help, and how you will handle the waiting. Agreeing this in advance makes the process much easier when a month passes without success.

The first months of trying

Use the calculator to estimate your fertile window, and confirm it with mucus and ovulation tests. Aim for the two to three days before ovulation, trying every other day across the window. Keep the routine sustainable: a plan you can repeat for six months beats an intense plan you abandon after one. Keep taking folic acid, keep sleeping well, and keep exercise moderate. Avoid turning every symptom into a sign of pregnancy, because the two-week wait is stressful enough without symptom-spotting.

If it takes longer than expected

Even with good timing, several months without success is normal: the chance per cycle is roughly one in four for a healthy couple in their 20s, and it falls with age. If you are under 35, seek advice after a year of trying; if you are 35 or older, after six months. Bring your cycle records to the appointment, because they show whether ovulation is happening and whether timing was right. The first checks are usually simple blood tests and a semen analysis, and many causes of delay are treatable.

Looking after yourselves

Trying to conceive can strain even a strong relationship, because it mixes hope, disappointment and a monthly deadline. Protect the parts of life that have nothing to do with trying: keep date nights, keep hobbies, and keep talking about things other than fertility. If the process becomes overwhelming, talk to your doctor or a counsellor, because support is part of the plan, not a sign of failure. A calm, well-prepared start gives you the best chance, and it also makes the journey itself easier to live through.

A checklist for the three months before trying

Three months before trying, start folic acid and book a health check. Two months before, review medicines, check vaccinations, and begin tracking cycles so you know your average length and ovulation pattern. One month before, stop hormonal contraception if you use it, keep tracking, and talk with your partner about the plan: how you will time things, how long you will try before seeking help, and how you will handle the waiting. In the first month of trying, use the calculator and body signs to time the fertile window, and keep the routine sustainable. Tick each step off as you go, because a plan you can see is easier to follow than one you carry in your head, and it keeps the process calm rather than rushed.

Tip: Start folic acid at least a month before you try to conceive, and start tracking your cycle three months before — by the time you start, you will already know your pattern.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start folic acid?

At least a month before trying to conceive, and keep taking it through the first weeks of pregnancy. Ask a doctor or pharmacist for the right dose.

How long is it normal to try before conceiving?

Several months is normal. The chance per cycle is roughly one in four for a healthy couple in their 20s. Seek advice after a year, or six months if you are 35 or older.

Should I stop contraception months in advance?

For the pill, patch or ring, fertility usually returns within weeks, so stopping a month or two before trying is enough. The injection takes longer to wear off.
