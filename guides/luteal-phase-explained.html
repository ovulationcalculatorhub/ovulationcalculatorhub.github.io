The Luteal Phase Explained

What the luteal phase is, why it lasts 12 to 16 days, and what a short or long luteal phase can mean.

The luteal phase is the second half of your menstrual cycle, running from ovulation to the start of your next period. It is the most consistent part of the cycle, usually lasting 12 to 16 days, and its steadiness is the reason ovulation can be estimated as 14 days before your next period. Understanding it helps you read your cycle, spot when something is off, and know when a late period really means a late ovulation.

What happens in the luteal phase

After the egg is released, the empty follicle transforms into a structure called the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. Progesterone keeps the lining of the uterus thick and ready, raises your basal body temperature by about 0.3 to 0.5 degrees Celsius, and thickens cervical mucus. If the egg is fertilised and implants, the corpus luteum keeps producing progesterone to support the early pregnancy. If no pregnancy occurs, the corpus luteum breaks down after about two weeks, progesterone falls, and the uterine lining sheds as your next period.

Why the length is so steady

The luteal phase is steady because it is driven by the lifespan of the corpus luteum, which is roughly fixed at about 14 days. That is very different from the follicular phase before ovulation, which can stretch or shrink with stress, illness or travel. This is why cycle length varies mostly because ovulation moves, not because the luteal phase changes. If your cycle is 35 days long, it is usually because ovulation came late, not because the luteal phase ran long. Knowing this lets you work backwards: subtract 14 days from your next period to estimate ovulation.

What a short luteal phase means

A luteal phase shorter than 10 days is called a short luteal phase, and it can matter for conception, because the uterine lining may start to shed before a fertilised egg has time to implant. A single short luteal phase is common and usually not a problem, especially after stress or illness. If it happens repeatedly over several cycles, it is worth discussing with a doctor, because it can be linked to low progesterone, thyroid issues, or high prolactin. Tracking your temperature gives you the data: the number of days from the temperature rise to your next period is your luteal phase length.

What a long luteal phase means

A luteal phase longer than 17 days can be an early sign of pregnancy, because the corpus luteum keeps going when implantation occurs. It can also happen occasionally without pregnancy, for example after a late ovulation that the calendar misread. If your period is more than a few days late and a pregnancy test is negative, the most likely explanation is that ovulation came later than usual, which pushes the whole schedule back. Wait a few more days and test again if your period still has not arrived, and see a doctor if periods stop for three months.

How to measure your own luteal phase

You need two dates: the day of ovulation and the first day of your next period. Ovulation day can be estimated from a sustained temperature rise — the day before the rise is usually ovulation day — or from a positive ovulation test, which comes about a day before ovulation. Count the days from ovulation to the day before your next period. Do this for three cycles and average the result. A steady 12 to 16 days is normal. Keep the record, because it is exactly the information a doctor will ask for if you ever seek advice about your cycle.

Working out your own luteal phase

To measure your luteal phase, you need two dates for each of three cycles: the day of ovulation and the first day of your next period. Find ovulation from a sustained temperature rise, counting the day before the rise as ovulation day, or from a positive ovulation test, which comes about a day before ovulation. Count the days from ovulation to the day before your next period: that is your luteal phase for that cycle. Repeat for three cycles and average the result. If the average is 12 to 16 days, your luteal phase is normal. If it is under 10 days in all three cycles, mention it to a doctor, because it can affect implantation. A single short phase after stress or illness is common and usually not a concern.

Tip: Count backwards, not forwards: your luteal phase is the steady 12 to 16 days between ovulation and your next period, so ovulation is about 14 days before your period, not 14 days after your last one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a normal luteal phase?

Usually 12 to 16 days. The average is about 14 days, and it is the most consistent part of the cycle.

What does a short luteal phase mean?

Under 10 days, repeated over several cycles, it can make implantation harder. A single short phase after stress is usually not a concern.

Can a long luteal phase mean pregnancy?

A luteal phase over 17 days can be an early sign of pregnancy. If your period is late and a test is negative, ovulation was probably later than usual.
